Assured autonomy

Everything so far assumed a human decides what the system does. Autonomous systems (drones, vehicles, robots, and now AI agents) decide for themselves, often with a neural network in the loop that nobody can fully inspect. How do you trust that?

Core idea You can't prove a neural network is always right. So you don't trust the model; you trust the architecture around it. Define what “safe” means, verify what you can, bound what you can't with runtime monitors and simple, verifiable fallbacks, and tie the evidence together in an explicit argument for why the system is safe enough.

8.1Why autonomy is hard to assure

Traditional software assurance works by specifying behavior and testing or proving the code meets it. Learning-enabled autonomy breaks that in three ways:

  • The input space can't be enumerated. A camera sees effectively infinite scenes. Testing samples a vanishing fraction.
  • The behavior isn't written down. A neural network's decision logic is millions of learned weights, not reviewable rules. Chapter 01's “stack of promises” has a layer with no explicit promise.
  • The world shifts. Fog, a new road layout, or a sensor aging all put the system outside its training distribution, often without it knowing.

Add an adversary and it gets worse. Chapter 06's evasion attacks become physical: stickers that make a stop sign read as a speed limit, GPS spoofing, and LiDAR spoofing. For autonomous systems, safety and security converge, because an attacker's goal is often to make the system do something unsafe.

8.2The assurance toolkit

1 · Specify: what does “safe” mean?

Before anything can be verified, it has to be stated. Safety properties (“never closer than 2 m to a pedestrian,” “never leave the geofence”) and an operational design domain (ODD), the conditions the system is designed for (daylight, highways, under 65 mph). Much of assurance is admitting what's out of scope and detecting when you've left it.

2 · Verify: prove what you can

Formal methods (model checking, theorem proving) prove properties over all behaviors of a model, which is exhaustive but hard to scale. Neural network verification tools (for example, Marabou or α,β-CROWN) can prove bounded properties, such as “no perturbation within ε of this input changes the classification,” for small networks and regions. Simulation and scenario-based testing cover the rest, with falsification actively searching for inputs that break the system (fuzzing for autonomy).

3 · Bound at runtime: the Simplex architecture

This is the idea I'd put on a slide. Run the high-performance, unverifiable AI controller and a simple, verifiable safety controller side by side. A monitor watches the system state. As long as it's safely inside a recoverable envelope, the AI drives. When the AI's actions would push it toward the edge, the monitor switches to the safety controller before a violation happens. You get the AI's performance most of the time and a proof-backed guarantee all of the time. This is runtime assurance (RTA), used in aviation (ASTM F3269 covers it for unmanned aircraft) and in research on autonomous vehicles.

The monitor predicts one step ahead: if position plus velocity × lookahead crosses the envelope, it switches. The safety controller is dumb, slow, and verifiable. That's the point: you only need to prove the simple parts.

4 · Argue: the assurance case

No single technique covers everything, so the evidence is assembled into an assurance case: a structured argument that a top-level claim (“the system is acceptably safe in its ODD”) is supported by sub-claims, each backed by evidence such as test results, proofs, and analyses. The usual notation is GSN (Goal Structuring Notation). The value is the same as a threat model: it makes the reasoning reviewable and exposes where the evidence is thin. Standards like UL 4600 (autonomous products), ISO 26262 (functional safety), and ISO 21448/SOTIF (hazards from a function's limitations, not failures) are built around this style of argument.

8.3Why this matters beyond robots: AI agents

LLM agents that browse, write code, and call APIs are autonomous systems too: sense (read context), decide (the model), act (tools). The assurance playbook transfers almost directly, and it's where I expect this course to pay off in AppSec work:

  • ODD → scope: define which tasks and data the agent is designed for, and refuse or escalate outside that.
  • Simplex → guardrails: a deterministic policy layer between the model and its tools that allowlists actions, enforces the user's permissions, and requires approval for irreversible steps. The model proposes; simple, verifiable code disposes.
  • Runtime monitors → observability: log and rate-limit tool calls, and detect anomalous action sequences.
  • Assurance case → security review: an explicit argument for why this agent can't be driven into harm by prompt injection, and what evidence supports it.
Where it breaks
  • Unknown unknowns: testing and even the ODD are defined by what designers imagined. The dangerous cases are the ones nobody did.
  • Monitors are software too: a runtime monitor that trusts spoofed sensor data switches on the wrong facts. The trust boundary moves; it doesn't vanish.
  • Assurance cases as paperwork: an argument written to justify a decision already made is confirmation bias with diagrams.
  • Automation complacency: humans “in the loop” stop paying attention when the system is usually right.
On the job (AppSec)
  • Reviewing agentic AI features with a runtime-assurance mindset: what's the safety envelope, and what enforces it outside the model?
  • Designing deterministic guardrails (policy engines, allowlists, approval gates) as the verifiable part of an unverifiable system.
  • Writing security assurance arguments for high-risk launches, showing claims, evidence, and known gaps.
  • Roles in autonomous vehicles, drones, defense, and robotics where safety and security engineering overlap.

Check yourself

What is the Simplex architecture, and why does it work?

A complex, high-performance controller runs alongside a simple, verified safety controller. A decision module switches to the safety controller when the state approaches the boundary of a region from which safe recovery is guaranteed. It works because you only need to verify the simple parts (safety controller plus switching logic), not the complex one.

What is an operational design domain?

The specific conditions a system is designed to operate in, such as geography, weather, speed, and time of day. Safety claims are only made within the ODD, so detecting ODD exit and handing off safely is itself a safety requirement.

How is assuring an AI system different from testing traditional software?

The behavior is learned rather than specified, the input space can't be enumerated, and performance degrades under distribution shift in ways that are hard to predict. So assurance leans on runtime monitoring, bounded verification, out-of-distribution detection, and structured arguments, rather than only requirements-based testing.

How do safety and security interact in autonomous systems?

A security attack (spoofed GPS, an adversarial patch, a hijacked command) can directly cause a safety hazard, and safety mechanisms can be attack surface (tricking a monitor into triggering a fallback as denial of service). They need joint analysis, often called security-informed safety.
