Thinking like security

The first real security course didn't teach a tool. It taught a question: secure against whom, protecting what, at what cost? Everything later in the degree is an answer to that question for one specific domain.

Core idea “Secure” is not a property a system has. It's a claim about a system, against a specific adversary, protecting specific assets, at an acceptable cost. The job is making that claim explicit, then finding where it's false.

2.1What we're protecting: CIA, plus two

The CIA triad is the vocabulary every security conversation uses, so it's worth being precise:

  • Confidentiality: only the right parties can read it. Breaks via: data leaks, broken access control, unencrypted traffic.
  • Integrity: it hasn't been changed without authorization. Breaks via: tampering, injection, supply-chain compromise.
  • Availability: it's there when needed. Breaks via: DDoS, ransomware, and the expired certificate that takes production down.

Two more earn their place in practice: authenticity (it really came from who it claims) and non-repudiation (they can't later deny sending it). Both come back in Chapters 03 and 04, where digital signatures provide them.

Then there's the triple that makes access decisions work, sometimes called AAA: authentication (who are you?), authorization (what are you allowed to do?), and accounting/audit (what did you do?). Hold on to the difference between the first two. Most of application security's worst bugs are authorization bugs in systems with perfectly fine authentication.

2.2Principles that don't age

In 1975, Saltzer and Schroeder wrote down design principles for protection systems. Fifty years later they still read like a modern code-review checklist:

Least privilegeEvery component gets only the access it needs, for only as long as it needs it.
Fail-safe defaultsDeny by default. Access is granted explicitly, never assumed.
Complete mediationCheck authorization on every access, not just the first one or the one on the UI.
Economy of mechanismKeep security code small and simple enough to actually review.
Open designSecurity must not depend on the design being secret (Kerckhoffs's principle, restated).
Separation of privilegeCritical actions need more than one key: two people, two factors.
Least common mechanismMinimize shared state between users; shared things become channels.
Psychological acceptabilityIf security is painful, people route around it. Usability is a control.

Add two modern restatements. Defense in depth: assume any single control will fail, so layer them, so that one mistake isn't a breach. Zero trust: network location grants nothing, so every request is authenticated and authorized on its own merits. Zero trust is really complete mediation applied to networks.

2.3Threat modeling: the core skill

If this degree gave me one transferable skill, it's this. Threat modeling is structured paranoia, and Adam Shostack's four questions are the whole method:

  1. What are we working on? Draw the system as a data-flow diagram: processes, data stores, external entities, and the flows between them.
  2. What can go wrong? Walk each flow, especially where it crosses a trust boundary, and apply a checklist like STRIDE.
  3. What are we going to do about it? Mitigate, eliminate, transfer, or accept each threat.
  4. Did we do a good job? Validate: test it, review it, revisit it when the design changes.

2.4Risk: deciding what matters

A threat model produces a long list. Risk management turns it into priorities. The classic formulation is risk = likelihood × impact. It's crude, but it forces the right conversation. A dramatic threat that's nearly impossible to pull off can matter less than a boring one that's trivial to exploit.

You then choose a treatment: mitigate (add a control), avoid (don't build the risky feature), transfer (insurance, or a vendor carries it), or accept (documented, owned, and revisited). Controls themselves come in flavors: preventive (MFA), detective (alerting), and corrective (backups, incident response). Good programs use all three, because prevention eventually fails.

Where it breaks
  • Security as an absolute: teams that chase “secure” with no threat model spend money on the wrong controls.
  • Checking once: authorization enforced in the UI but not the API violates complete mediation. That's the most common serious web bug (Chapter 05).
  • Implicit trust: “it's on the internal network” or “only our service calls this endpoint” are trust assumptions nobody wrote down.
  • Threat models that rot: a diagram from two years ago describes a system that no longer exists.
On the job (AppSec)
  • Design reviews are threat modeling. An AppSec engineer's highest-leverage hour is the one spent on a design doc before code exists.
  • Finding severity is risk. CVSS scores the vulnerability, but you adjust for context: is it reachable? Is the data sensitive? Is there a compensating control?
  • Saying yes safely. The goal is enabling the feature with the right controls, not blocking it.
Say it in 60 seconds

“My foundation is that ‘secure’ only means something relative to a threat, an asset, and a cost. So my core method is threat modeling. I diagram the data flows, mark the trust boundaries, and walk each crossing with something like STRIDE: spoofing, tampering, repudiation, disclosure, denial of service, and privilege escalation. Then I rank what I find by likelihood and impact and pick a treatment. The principles underneath are old and durable: least privilege, deny by default, check authorization on every request, and layer defenses because any single control will eventually fail.”

Check yourself

What's the difference between authentication and authorization, and which fails more often?

Authentication proves identity; authorization decides what that identity may do. Authorization fails far more often in practice, because it's scattered across every endpoint and object, while authentication is usually centralized in one well-tested library or identity provider. That's why Broken Access Control is #1 on the OWASP Top 10.

Walk me through how you'd threat model a new feature.

Use the four questions. Draw the data-flow diagram with trust boundaries. Enumerate threats at each boundary crossing (STRIDE). Rank them by likelihood and impact. Agree on mitigations with the team and turn them into tickets or tests. Revisit when the design changes. Keep it lightweight: a whiteboard and 45 minutes beats a 40-page document nobody reads.

What is defense in depth? Give a concrete example.

Layered, independent controls, so one failure isn't a breach. For SQL injection: parameterized queries (prevent), a least-privilege DB account (limit blast radius), a WAF (partial filter), and query anomaly alerting (detect). Any single one might fail; together they're resilient.

What does “zero trust” actually mean?

No implicit trust based on network location. Every request is authenticated, authorized, and encrypted on its own merits, using identity and device posture, with least privilege. It replaces the “hard shell, soft center” perimeter model, which fails the moment an attacker gets inside.
