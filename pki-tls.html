PKI & TLS

Encryption answers “can anyone else read this?” The harder question is “who am I actually talking to?” Public key infrastructure is the internet's answer, and it's as much an operations discipline as a cryptographic one.

Core idea Key exchange gives you a secret shared with someone. PKI tells you who. It works by delegated vouching: a small set of roots you already trust vouch for intermediates, which vouch for sites. The chain is only as strong as its weakest link and the operational discipline of everyone who holds a key.

4.1The problem PKI solves

Chapter 03 ended with a gap. Diffie-Hellman lets two parties agree on a secret over an open channel, but if an attacker sits in the middle, you've securely agreed on a key with the attacker. Encryption without authentication is a private conversation with a stranger.

A certificate closes the gap. It's a signed statement: “public key K belongs to bank.example, valid until this date, signed by a certificate authority.” If you trust the CA's signing key, and the signature verifies, you can trust the binding. And how do you trust the CA? Another certificate, up to a root that ships preinstalled in your OS or browser's trust store. There are only a few hundred roots, and browsers and OS vendors (their “root programs”) police them hard.

    4.4Revocation, transparency, and the move to short lives

    Revocation is PKI's historically weak spot. If a private key leaks, the certificate should stop being trusted before it expires. CRLs (published lists) got huge, and OCSP (live queries) was slow, leaked browsing history to the CA, and usually “soft-failed,” meaning that if the check couldn't be completed, browsers connected anyway. The industry's answer has been to make certificates expire before revocation matters. Let's Encrypt shut down its OCSP service in 2025.

    In April 2025, the CA/Browser Forum passed Ballot SC-081 to shrink the maximum public TLS certificate lifetime in steps: 398 days → 200 days (March 2026) → 100 days (March 2027) → 47 days (March 2029). Domain-validation reuse drops to 10 days. At 47 days, manual renewal becomes impossible at scale. That's the point: the industry is forcing automation (ACME, cert-manager) and crypto agility, which the post-quantum migration will need.

    Certificate Transparency fixed a different problem: a CA mis-issuing a certificate for your domain. Every public certificate must now be logged in append-only, Merkle-tree-backed public logs (Chapter 01's data structure), and browsers require proof of logging. Anyone, including you, can monitor the logs for certificates issued for your domains (for example, with crt.sh).

    Where it breaks
    • CA compromise: in 2011 DigiNotar was breached and issued a fraudulent *.google.com certificate that was used to intercept traffic in Iran. The CA was distrusted and went bankrupt. CT exists largely because of cases like this.
    • Expired certificates: the most common PKI failure is operational. Equifax's 2017 breach went unnoticed for months partly because an expired certificate on a traffic-inspection device meant encrypted exfiltration wasn't being inspected.
    • Validation turned off: verify=False, InsecureSkipVerify: true, and trust-all TrustManagers. One line in code silently cancels all of PKI.
    • Private PKI sprawl: internal CAs with no inventory, 10-year certificates, and keys on disk.
    On the job (AppSec)
    • Code review: grep for disabled certificate verification. It's a Critical finding hiding in “temporary” test code.
    • Service-to-service identity: mTLS in service meshes, workload identity (SPIFFE), and short-lived certificates instead of shared API keys.
    • Supply chain: code signing and artifact signing (Sigstore/cosign) are PKI applied to software. Verify signatures in the deploy pipeline, not just at build.
    • Certificate lifecycle: inventory, automated renewal, and expiry monitoring. The 47-day schedule makes this everybody's problem.
    Say it in 60 seconds

    “PKI answers the question encryption can't: who am I talking to? A certificate binds a public key to a name, signed by a CA, and trust flows down from a small set of roots in the OS trust store through intermediates to the site. The browser checks the signatures, validity dates, hostname, and revocation status. TLS 1.3 puts it together in one round trip: an ephemeral key exchange for forward secrecy, the certificate to prove identity, and symmetric AEAD for the data. What I took away is that PKI fails operationally far more than cryptographically: expired certificates, disabled verification, compromised CAs. That's why the industry is moving to 47-day certificates, full automation, and transparency logs.”

    Check yourself

    What happens, step by step, when I type https://bank.example into a browser?

    DNS resolves the name, then a TCP (or QUIC) connection is opened. The TLS 1.3 ClientHello goes out with a key share and SNI. The server replies with its key share, and both derive handshake keys. The server sends its certificate chain and a signature over the handshake transcript. The browser validates the chain to a trusted root, checks dates and hostname, and verifies the signature. Both sides exchange Finished messages and derive traffic keys. HTTP then flows over AES-GCM or ChaCha20.

    What's the difference between TLS and mTLS?

    In ordinary TLS only the server presents a certificate. In mutual TLS the client does too, so both sides are authenticated cryptographically. It's common for service-to-service traffic in zero-trust architectures and service meshes.

    Why are certificate lifetimes getting shorter?

    Revocation doesn't work reliably, so shorter lifetimes limit how long a compromised or mis-issued certificate stays useful. They also force automation and crypto agility, which the post-quantum migration will require. The CA/B Forum schedule goes 200 days (2026), 100 days (2027), then 47 days (2029).

    What does Certificate Transparency protect against?

    Mis-issuance: a CA (compromised, coerced, or careless) issuing a certificate for your domain to someone else. Because every public certificate must be logged publicly, domain owners can detect this, and browsers reject certificates without proof of logging.
