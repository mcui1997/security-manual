Chapter 07 · The business

Security in the organization

Every earlier chapter assumed someone would fund the fix. This one is about how that actually happens: risk expressed in the business's language, frameworks that organize the work, and the gap between being compliant and being secure.

Core idea Inside a company, security is a business-risk function. A technical risk that can't be translated into money, operations, legal exposure, or reputation doesn't get prioritized. And compliance is a floor, not a ceiling: it proves you did the required things, not that you're safe.

7.1Speaking the language

The same finding needs two descriptions: one precise enough for the engineer who fixes it, and one that lets a leader weigh it against everything else competing for the same budget. The executive version isn't dumbed down. It's translated into impact, likelihood, cost, and a decision.

The pattern for the exec version: what could happen → how likely → what it costs → what we recommend and what it costs to fix → what decision we need from you.

7.2Putting a number on it

Quantitative risk analysis is often too precise to be accurate, but the classic model is worth knowing because it structures the argument. Single loss expectancy (SLE) = asset value × exposure factor. Annualized loss expectancy (ALE) = SLE × annual rate of occurrence (ARO). A control is worth buying when the reduction in ALE exceeds its annual cost. (Modern programs often use FAIR, which does the same thing with ranges and distributions instead of point estimates.)

The inputs are guesses, and that's fine. The value is in making assumptions explicit so people can argue about the right numbers instead of arguing about feelings.

7.3The frameworks, and how they fit together

There are a lot of acronyms, but they sort into three kinds. Frameworks organize a program. Control catalogs list what to do. Compliance regimes are what an auditor or regulator will check. 7.4Compliance is not security

Compliance tells you a control exists and was tested at a point in time. Security asks whether the control actually stops the adversary today. They overlap, and compliance is genuinely useful: it forces baselines, funds programs, and creates evidence. But organizations regularly pass audits and get breached anyway, because audits sample, checklists lag the threat, and a control that exists on paper (say, “we review access quarterly”) can be performed badly in practice. The healthy stance: build for security, and let compliance be the evidence that falls out of it.

7.5Operations: what happens when something goes wrong

A security operations center (SOC) collects telemetry (logs into a SIEM, endpoint data from EDR), turns it into alerts with detection rules often mapped to ATT&CK, and automates repetitive response with SOAR. Incident response follows the NIST lifecycle (SP 800-61, revised in 2025 to align with CSF 2.0): prepare → detect & analyze → contain, eradicate & recover → learn. The step organizations skip is the last one, the blameless post-incident review, which is where the program actually improves. The metrics everyone tracks are mean time to detect (MTTD) and mean time to respond (MTTR), because dwell time is damage.

Where it breaks
  • Checkbox security: optimizing for the audit instead of the adversary.
  • Risk accepted by nobody: a risk with no named owner and no review date isn't accepted; it's ignored.
  • Fear-driven asks: “we could get hacked” without likelihood, impact, or options loses credibility fast.
  • Untested plans: an incident response plan nobody has exercised fails on first contact. Tabletop exercises exist for this.
On the job (AppSec)
  • Severity with context: turning a CVSS score into a business priority (reachability, data sensitivity, exposure).
  • Evidence for audits: SOC 2 and PCI ask for secure SDLC evidence, like SAST in CI, code review, and vulnerability SLAs. AppSec owns much of it.
  • Risk exceptions: writing up a risk acceptance with an owner, compensating controls, and an expiry date.
  • Incident support: when the incident is an application vulnerability, AppSec finds the root cause, scopes the impact, and ships the fix.
  • Security questionnaires: answering customers' “do you do X?” honestly and consistently.

Check yourself

What are the six functions of NIST CSF 2.0?

Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover. Govern was added in 2.0 to cover strategy, roles, policy, risk appetite, and supply-chain risk management. It's the center that informs the other five.

What's the difference between a vulnerability, a threat, and a risk?

A vulnerability is a weakness (an unpatched server). A threat is something that could exploit it (a ransomware crew). Risk is the likelihood and impact of the threat exploiting the vulnerability against something you value. No threat or no valuable asset means little risk, even with a vulnerability.

How would you convince leadership to fund a security project?

Tie it to a business outcome they already care about: revenue (enterprise deals need SOC 2), regulatory exposure, or operational resilience. Quantify the risk with honest ranges. Present options with costs, including doing nothing. Make a clear recommendation and ask for a decision. Avoid fear-mongering and jargon.

What does a good risk acceptance look like?

It includes a clear description of the risk and its potential impact, a named business owner with the authority to accept it, any compensating controls, and an expiry or review date, after which it's re-evaluated rather than silently permanent.
